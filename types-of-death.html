Types of Death in Games

A long time ago, when games were played on machines at arcades, a player deposited money into the machine and then received a number of lives, sometimes 3, sometimes one. This created a highly focused player, invested in the game literally and metaphorically. While in the modern age of gaming, there are some game archetypes that continue along a similar vein, the industry largely has transitioned into different means of monetization and player retention. I propose to capture that original thrill of one coin = one life and transition it properly into the age of modern gaming.

The main features at play here are the "respawn" and the "health bar". The respawn means that death has been acknowledged, but not accepted, usually something happens to the game score or advantage balance, and the dead player is restored to a "spawn point", usually a safe location where they can regain their bearings before reentering the game. Yeah, like how salmon spawn. The health bar is like a meter or warning of how close death is, as the health bar lowers, the player experiences more pressure, excitement, anxiety, building until the health bar is restored, the conflict resolved, or the player dies. I am to rethink the respawn, and change the user experience for the health bar.

Different games have varying degrees of punishment for death, ranging from just being briefly timed out from the battlefield, granting points to the enemy team, granting greater statistical advantage to the enemy team, etc. In some "hardcore" versions of popular Massively Multiplayer Online games, such as Runescape, World of Warcraft, and Minecraft, the player will have their character, and all the progress they made, deleted upon death. Their position and name is immortalized on the leaderboard, and they start all over again.

Like I said earlier, this is how games used to be. You play Tetris, Pac-Man, Metal-Slug, Pinball, for as long as you can, hogging the machine, people gathering around, jeering at you, praying on your downfall so that they can have a turn at the game next. That rising adrenaline and pressure is what separates the gamers from the people who like games. And there is nothing wrong with people who like games, but the mainstream games industry will always look for the greatest open market share to capture. That is why Dark Souls was such a magnificent upset: it was intentionally hard, thrilling, and punished you for your deaths. And, that is why Elden Ring was the greatest selling Miyazaki game, over Sekiro and Dark Souls 3: it is easy mode. While the point can be made that they rectified their normie positive approach by making the DLC harder than expected, the point still stands that gamers are not the ones that the biggest game studios in the world are making games for.

Monetization of Death

I think I have a monetization strategy, centered around death, that can take the middle road and advantages from both the old school and the new, from the gamers and those who like games. Here's how it works:

  1. Player characters will have a variety of stats, such as = intelligence (INT), stamina (STA), dexterity (DEX), and strength (STR). These stats will be applied, on a consistent basis, based on time spent and concurrence of activities in game.
    1. If a player spends a second running in game, they will get 0.000001 xp for STA
    2. If a player parries a sword, they will get 0.01 xp for DEX
  2. When making a new character, a player can select from a few presets. No preset is to have an expressly intimidating advantage over the others, only to save some inconvenience of skipping the tutorial level, or avoiding the tricky initial stages of getting your first strength or intelligence point.
    1. $0 free: uneducated wretch
      1. level 1 stats
      2. tutorial level
    2. $1: fledgling
      1. maybe 10 random stats distribution
      2. tutorial quest complete
    3. $3: incentivized job
      1. basic 2 hour stat distribution for the job:
        1. mercenary: 2 INT, 5 DEX, 3 STR, 3 STA
        2. guardsman: 1 INT, 3 DEX, 3 STR, 7 STA
    4. $5: directed stats application, sorcerer, warrior, merchant, etc., has some job
    5. $99: high charisma or money: aristocrat, established character
  3. When the player dies, their character lies dead at that location, with their body accessible to other players. Their items can be taken, their spells pilfered, their assets and housing available to be purchased.

First note that in this game there must be many ways to avoid dying, especially for the easier levels. As the player advances higher, dying will be more likely. However, it still must be possible to die at any time. This will do a few things: one, keep it exciting. Two, generate income. Three, promote account selling. Yes, the game will allow and even facilitate the trading of accounts. By placing a monetary value on levels and items, we can create a source of income for players, who can level accounts and then legally trade them to other players. We can fund the game by taxing this marketplace, or by supplying some basic characters ourselves, like the fledgling, sorcerer, warrior, and aristocrat mentioned above.

This is my version of putting a coin in the machine. I think it has the potential to give players the excitement of a hardcore game mode, while at the same time reward them for their effort by creating value in reaching certain game thresholds.

In response to the Devil's Advocate's valid concerns about the potential for exploitation, toxicity, and perceived pay-to-win mechanics in our game concept, I believe there are strategic ways to mitigate these risks while preserving the core high-stakes, high-reward gameplay loop.

The key is to carefully design the character class and progression system to incentivize a diverse array of playstyles and motivations, not just the most ruthless or financially advantaged. By offering "discount deals" on certain character archetypes like merchant marines, soldiers, and guardians, we can attract players who are drawn to more honorable or service-oriented roles.

These starter classes could have lower barriers to entry, with reduced stat requirements and perhaps even some built-in safeguards or insurances against the harshest consequences of death. This would give players a more accessible pathway to engage with the core gameplay loop without feeling constantly under threat of having their hard-earned progress wiped out.

At the same time, we can reserve the more premium, high-risk/high-reward character options for players who truly crave the thrill of the pirate or mercenary lifestyle. These classes could have steeper stat requirements, more severe death penalties, and potentially even a higher cost of entry. But crucially, their advantages should be meaningful enough to attract a dedicated player base without overshadowing the more accessible options.

The key is to create a diverse ecosystem of character types and progression paths, each with their own unique value proposition. This will help foster a healthy player community with a range of motivations and playstyles, reducing the likelihood of a toxic, cutthroat environment where only the deepest pockets or most ruthless players can succeed.

Additionally, we can explore alternative monetization strategies that go beyond the direct trading of accounts and items. Cosmetic customization, optional insurance/protection services, and other ancillary offerings could provide alternative revenue streams that enhance the experience without directly impacting power dynamics or progression.

By thoughtfully balancing the high-stakes thrills of our core gameplay loop with strategic character design and monetization, we can capture the essence of the old-school arcade experience while mitigating the potential pitfalls. It's a delicate balance, to be sure, but one that I believe is achievable with the right approach - one that prioritizes player engagement, community health, and long-term sustainability.
